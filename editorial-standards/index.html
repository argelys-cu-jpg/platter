Preamble

The commitments this whole document lives under.

A publication that reviews meal services, ranks kitchen tools, and writes recipes is only worth reading if the reader can see, exactly, how the sausage is made. This document is that view.

Everything below is enforceable. Editors and freelancers sign onto it before they file. Reviews, guides, stories, and recipes that violate anything in this document are held, corrected, or unpublished — never quietly softened.

01
How we test meal services

Every service earns its score across a full week of real dinners.

A meal service does not get a Platter score after one tasting. It gets a score after the full editorial cycle: one week of ordering, one week of cooking through the box, and one week where the tester deliberately treats the service the way a distracted household would.

Every service is bought at retail with an editorial credit card. We do not accept comped subscriptions, comped meals, or trial codes with different terms than the ones a reader would see at signup.

  • A Same tester, three weeks. One reviewer runs the same service across three consecutive weeks — no menu cherry-picking, no first-week honeymoon.
  • B Two households. Every score is confirmed by a second household of a different size and cooking level. Disagreements are noted in the review, not averaged away.
  • C Retail credit card. Reviews are bought at retail. No comps. No PR pack. No 'special editorial pricing.'
  • D The Wednesday rule. A service that only tastes good on delivery day, hot from the oven, does not pass. Wednesday reheat quality is scored, not optional.
02
How we score

Three columns: Plate, Fit, Clarity.

Every Platter score is a weighted average of three columns — Plate (flavor, freshness, reheat), Fit (ordering flow, portions, real-week compatibility), and Clarity (pricing, cancellation, honest limitations). A perfect ten in one column cannot offset a three in another.

Reviewers submit scores independently before comparing notes. The final published score is the reviewer's, not a committee's — and it appears on the piece with the reviewer's name attached to it.

  • 1 Plate — 45%. Flavor, texture, freshness on day three, sauce integrity, and reheat quality on a real weeknight.
  • 2 Fit — 35%. Ordering flow, filters, portions, cleanup, and how the meal actually works on a Tuesday.
  • 3 Clarity — 20%. Pricing, cancellation, limitations, and honest disclosure of who this service is not for.
03
How the Platter Index moves

Rankings move when a service changes, not when the news cycle does.

The Platter Index is a live ranking. It moves when a service does something that meaningfully changes its Plate, Fit, or Clarity score — a menu overhaul, a portion change, a pricing shift, a delivery footprint expansion, a genuine drop in quality.

The Index does not move on funding announcements, celebrity chef signings, ad campaigns, or PR pressure. If a service asks to be re-evaluated, we may re-review — but only through the same tester + three-week + retail-credit-card process.

On the record

We have re-reviewed a service at its request four times in the last two years. Two moved up. One moved down. One stayed put. The service that dropped was not consulted before publication, and we did not accept its request to withhold the piece.

04
Recipe testing

Every recipe is cooked three times before it publishes.

Recipes on the Platter desk go through a three-pass test before they ship. First pass is written by the recipe developer, on a real weeknight, at home. Second pass is a cold cross-test by a second cook, from the recipe card alone. Third pass is the rescue test — we deliberately swap, sub, and shortcut it, and only publish if it still lands.

  • P1 First pass. The developer writes the recipe once, on a real Tuesday night, with real fatigue.
  • P2 Cross-test. A second cook makes it from the recipe card alone — no phone calls, no clarifications.
  • P3 Rescue test. We deliberately swap ingredients, cut a shortcut, and shift a technique. If it still works, it publishes.
05
Guides

A guide is not a listicle. It is a system you can run.

Every guide on Platter earns four things before it ships: it was written by a working cook, tested end-to-end in a real kitchen, edited by an editor who cooked from it, and survived the Tuesday-night test — where every step has a rescue move for the night that goes sideways.

Guides are dated. If a guide is more than 18 months old, it is either refreshed by the original author or clearly marked as an archived version at the top of the page.

06
Reporting standards

Sourcing, fact-checking, and the on-the-record rule.

Every reported piece on the Stories desk names its sources on the record whenever possible. Anonymous sourcing is used only when a source faces real professional risk, and every anonymous source in a Platter piece is known by name to at least one editor other than the reporter.

Every reported piece is fact-checked against primary documents by a second party before it publishes. Fact-checking is a separate role from editing, and never done by the reporter themselves.

  • 1 Named sources by default. Anonymous quotes are the exception, disclosed, and never used to attack a competitor of a Platter partner.
  • 2 Second-party fact-check. Every reported piece is checked by a fact-checker who is not the reporter or the editor of record.
  • 3 Subject notification. Any named business or person in a critical piece is contacted for comment, in writing, at least 48 hours before publication.
07
Conflicts of interest

Nobody on the desk reviews something they have a stake in.

Every editor and staff freelancer discloses financial and personal ties to services, brands, and chefs annually. Anyone with a material tie to a service is recused from writing, editing, and scoring that service.

This is enforced. Two contributors have been recused from a review in the last year. One review was reassigned to a different desk. One piece was killed and not replaced.

Read this closely

If a Platter editor's spouse, sibling, or business partner works at a service being reviewed, that editor does not touch the piece. Period. This includes edit, layout, headline, and cover selection — the entire production chain.

08
Funding disclosures

The money, in order of size.

The full breakdown of Platter's funding sources — Batch, reader subscriptions, podcast sponsorship, and editorial partnerships — is disclosed on the About page and updated quarterly. No funder can influence a review, ranking, or story. Sponsors are disclosed on every episode or piece they underwrite.

  • 56% Batch subscription revenue. The planner subsidizes the newsroom. Batch never appears in a review, ranking, or comparison as a graded product.
  • 22% Reader subscriptions (Platter+). Optional. Every editorial page on this site is free. Members get archives, discounts on events, and priority for reader Q&As.
  • 14% Podcast + event sponsorship. Disclosed on every episode. Sponsors are chosen by the publisher, never by the editorial masthead, and never overlap with a ranked service.
  • 8% Editorial partnerships. A small, hand-picked set. Disclosed at the top of any piece they underwrite. No partner has veto or edit rights.
09
AI use in the newsroom

We do not use generative AI to write recipes, reviews, or reporting.

Recipes, reviews, guides, stories, and interviews on Platter are written by named human authors. We do not use generative AI to draft, ghostwrite, or auto-fill any editorial copy. Headlines and social copy also come from a human editor.

We do use small AI tools inside the workflow — transcription, spell-check, image resizing, and Batch's planning logic — and we say so, on the record. Batch's core planning logic is deterministic, not an LLM.

10
Changes to this document

How this document changes, and who has to sign off.

Any material change to this document requires sign-off from the full executive masthead — the editor in chief and the four desk executive editors — and is added to the public changelog with a version bump, a date, and a summary of what changed and why.

Non-material changes (typos, phone numbers, updated URLs) can be made by a single editor and are noted in the changelog under "minor edits." We do not silently update this page.

Reader complaints

When we get it wrong, tell us.

Reader complaints route to a real editor, not a shared inbox. If your note involves a factual error, we aim to respond within one business day and correct within three.

Factual error
corrections@platter.example Include the URL and a link or citation for the correct fact. We respond within one business day.
Standards concern
standards@platter.example For conflicts of interest, disclosure issues, or a review you believe crossed a line.
General note
hello@platter.example Everything else — questions, praise, kitchen photos, and the occasional threat about our take on chili crisp.
